## Load-bearing quotes (the canon)

### On narratives (why no slides)

**Q1 — Amazon on why narratives.**
> "We don't do PowerPoint (or any other slide-oriented) presentations at Amazon. Instead, we write narratively structured six-page memos. We silently read one at the start of each meeting in a kind of 'study hall.'"
— Amazon's 2017 Letter to Shareholders

**Q2 — Amazon on what memos force.**
> "The narrative structure of a good memo forces better thought and better understanding of what's more important than what, and how things are related. Powerpoint-style presentations somehow give permission to gloss over ideas, flatten out any sense of relative importance, and ignore the interconnectedness of ideas."
— Amazon S-Team email, June 9, 2004

**Q2b — Amazon on the cost difference.**
> "The reason writing a good four-page memo is harder than writing a 20-page PowerPoint is because the narrative structure of a good memo forces better thought and better understanding of what's more important than what, and how things are related."
— Amazon at the Forum on Leadership, 2018

### On the cost of quality

**Q3 — Amazon on the cost of a great memo.**
> "The great memos are written and re-written, shared with colleagues who are asked to improve the work, set aside for a couple of days, and then edited again with a fresh mind. They simply can't be done in a day or two."
> "When a memo isn't great, it's not the writer's inability to recognize the high standard, but instead a wrong expectation on scope: they mistakenly believe a high-standards, six-page memo can be written in one or two days or even a few hours, when really it might take a week or more!"
— Amazon's 2017 Letter

**The handstand metaphor.** The 2017 letter illustrates this with a story: an Amazon employee wants to learn a handstand in two weeks; the coach says six months of daily practice. People fail not because they can't recognize quality, but because they budgeted hours when they needed weeks.

### On the document as the meeting

**Q3b — Amazon on the meeting.**
> "I like a crisp document and a messy meeting."
— Amazon leadership, recounted in BusinessToday, 2023

The document does the precision work. The meeting is for unstructured pushback and decision.

**Q3c — Amazon's executive reading discipline** (paraphrased from *Working Backwards*; not a verbatim quote). Amazon's S-Team reads memos assuming each sentence is wrong until proven otherwise. The critique addresses the content, not the writer's motive.

### On the PR/FAQ structure

**Q4 — Bryar & Carr on PR vs FAQ.**
> "The PR gives the reader the highlights of the customer experience. The FAQ provides all the salient details of the customer experience as well as a clear-eyed and thorough assessment of how expensive and challenging it will be for the company to build the product or create the service."
— *Working Backwards*

**Q5 — Bryar & Carr on six pages.**
> "We know that people read complex information at the rough average of three minutes per page, which in turn defines the functional length of a written narrative as about six pages for a 60-minute meeting."
— *Working Backwards*

**Q6 — Bryar & Carr on the FAQ split.**
> "External FAQs are the ones you would expect to hear from the press or customers… Internal FAQs are the questions that your team and the executive leadership will ask."
— *Working Backwards*

**Q7 — Bill Carr on PR vs FAQ.**
> "The press release defines the customer, the problem, and the solution. The FAQ is where you figure out how to turn fiction into reality."
— LinkedIn, Oct 2024

**Q7b — Bill Carr on iteration.**
> "The problem with these forecasts is that they are always wrong. Expect to revise it many times as your thinking evolves."
— LinkedIn, Oct 2024

### On the decision moment

**Q8 — Limp on greenlight.**
> "As soon as we agree on that document, the decision is made. That project is green-lighted. The next step is to find a single threaded leader to run that project."
— Amazon Chronicles

**Q9 — Limp on iteration.**
> "It is very rare that I would see a working backwards document like that for any new product…that we would approve the first time we saw it. Normally it takes many iterations."
— Amazon Chronicles

### On the launch shape

**Q10 — AWS on MLP.**
> "We break our releases into 'minimum lovable products.' Each iteration addresses a significant customer need that they're excited to buy and use."
— AWS Executive Insights

### On rejection

**Q11 — Bryar & Carr on rejection-as-feature.**
> "The fact that most PR/FAQs don't get approved is a feature, not a bug … preserves your company's resources to build products that will yield the highest impact."
— *Working Backwards* via Commoncog

### On the room

> "At Amazon, after a brief exchange of greetings and chitchat, everyone sits at the table, and the room goes completely silent… A six-page document that everyone must read before discussion begins."
— *Working Backwards*, via Saloi

The exec question that surfaces most in the discussion phase: **"So what?"** (per Pai's book notes).

### Bryar himself, on the litmus test

> "If this write-up doesn't convince someone to buy the product, then it needs to be rewritten."
— Colin Bryar, paraphrasing the PR litmus test, Coda doc

## Drift guards (distilled from primary material)

12-item checklist the skill enforces. Each item ties to a primary-source claim above.

1. **Customer's voice, not company's.** The PR uses words the customer would actually say. (Q4 / Q5)
2. **One page for the PR.** If it spills, the idea isn't crisp. (Q5)
3. **Named, situated customer in the quote** — not "a developer." (Q4 + Bryar Coda)
4. **No marketing words:** "revolutionary," "best-in-class," "industry-leading," "world-class," "seamlessly." (theprfaq.com on Amazon writing culture)
5. **No bullets / tables / diagrams in the narrative body.** Topic sentences, connected paragraphs. (Q1 / Q2)
6. **External FAQ short. Internal FAQ is the bulk.** (Q4 / Q6)
7. **Internal FAQ must include "what could kill this."** Existential risks are part of the doc. (Q4 / Q7)
8. **The PR is the MLP test.** Below MLP = no compelling PR is writable. (Q10)
9. **Expect multiple drafts over days/weeks, not hours.** (Q3 + Q9)
10. **The doc is the meeting.** Written for silent reading at the top, not slide accompaniment. (Q1 / Q3b)
11. **The author is invisible.** No name on the doc. (theprfaq.com; canonical to Amazon)
12. **Every paragraph must survive the exec's "so what?"** (Pai)

## The 2004 origins

On June 9, 2004 an Amazon S-Team email banned slide presentations:
> "We don't do PowerPoint (or any other slide-oriented) presentations at Amazon."

The same email elaborated:
> "The narrative structure of a good memo forces better thought and better understanding of what's more important than what, and how things are related. Powerpoint-style presentations somehow give permission to gloss over ideas, flatten out any sense of relative importance, and ignore the interconnectedness of ideas."

The PRFAQ as a named, structured artifact emerged from this period. The Kindle is the most-cited origin point: the team realized they needed to write the launch press release first, and if no one would jump out of their chair to buy what it described, the product wasn't ready.

(CNBC reproduction of the email: https://www.cnbc.com/2018/04/23/what-jeff-bezos-learned-from-requiring-6-page-memos-at-amazon.html)
